Technique 01 · First Principles

Strip the portfolio down to bedrock — then refactor the framing.

Three questions: what does a recruiter actually do in 30 seconds · what's the smallest artifact that would force a hire · which one assumption, if false, changes everything.

QUESTION 01 · 30-SECOND SCAN

What does the recruiter actually read?

→ Summary of work and experience. So the hero, "what I actually do," and proof numbers have to compress to that scan — not just sit on top of it. The page has to survive being glanced at, not just read.

What if "get the interview" is the wrong goal?

→ Most portfolios aim to land the interview. The right framing is "make the interview feel redundant because the evidence is already on screen." This rearchitects the site from pitch to proof.

Technique 03 · Worst Possible Idea

The traps most portfolios fall into — and what they teach.

Worst-possible Sanjit portfolio, generated. Each "worst" gets flipped into the design principle it implies. Three temptations flagged as highest-value flips.

WORST · 01
47 tech logos with self-rating bars.
Self-rated "9/10 in React" reads as a LinkedIn profile circa 2014. It's also the easiest thing to fake — recruiters calibrate accordingly.
Replace ratings with evidence-shaped tokens: a case study, a pattern, a shipped artifact. No more rates — just receipts.
WORST · 02
"Built with [template]" in the footer.
Admitting a template signals "I bought this." It also collapses the distinction between you and the next person who bought the same template.
Build the structure itself — pattern library, content schema, recruiter mode — into the portfolio. Make the engineering of the site part of the proof.
WORST · 03
3,000-word About section.
Recruiters won't read it. Anyone who skims will close the page before reaching the part that matters (the proof). Length is not depth.
Compress identity into 1 sentence + 4 vectors + recruiter mode. Long-form prose lives only where it earns its place — case study decisions, leadership call transcripts.

Technique 04 · What If Scenarios

Detonate one constraint at a time —
the homepage decentralization unlock.

Four constraints detonated. The most consequential — "what if the recruiter never visits the homepage?" — surfaces an architectural consequence that reshapes the entire portfolio.

WHAT IF · #1 — CONSEQUENTIAL CHOSEN

The recruiter never visits the homepage?

Then every page must be a standalone proof surface, not a deep link from a hub. Decentralize the pitch: every page carries a 2-sentence positioning line, at least one proof number, and a path back to the rest of the site. The portfolio becomes a ring of rooms that all open to the outside, not a lobby with locked doors.

WHAT IF · #2 RESPECT

The site has to load on a 2G phone in Bangladesh?

Static-first + SSG is the only honest response. No client-side frameworks for content that doesn't need them. Interactive moments deferred, not absent — they load after the proof does.

WHAT IF · #3 DURABILITY

Sanjit disappears tomorrow?

Content sources survive. The CV file at cv.md remains the truth source; case studies live as markdown with frontmatter; the pattern library is additive and self-describing.

WHAT IF · #4 SURFACE

The only artifact is the GitHub?

The repo's README, commit log, and PR history have to carry proof too. They become supplementary proof surfaces, with recruiter-facing routing from repo → portfolio, not the other way.

The Architecture Review Pattern

How to own technical decision-making across a 7-person team without becoming a bottleneck. The CV mentions it in a single line: "managing delivery through Azure Boards and enforcing high code quality via reviews and mentoring" — this pattern is the how behind that line.

It's also the pattern that scales the CEO/recruiter argument: "he can do it — whatever you give him to build, he can build it for you, and better than others." Architecture Review is the demonstrated mechanism by which that claim is true.

Sample moves · from real work
1.Co-design review checklist with the team, not for them.
2.Define "blocking" sharply; reserve the right of refusal for cross-cutting risk.
3.Pair-review juniors weekly; lead-review seniors asynchronously.
4.Surface architecture decisions in writing — including the ones you rejected.
5.Time-box the bottleneck: every review has a 24-hour clock.

Decisions to carry forward

How to read this. Each decision was earned inside the session — surfaced through contradiction, picked over alternatives, and locked when the user said ship it. Cross out anything you no longer recognize as load-bearing.
Decision 01

Recruiter-Mode Toggle is the spine

A single view that compresses the portfolio into ammunition the recruiter can forward to a hiring manager. All evidence supports one sentence.

Decision 02

Pattern Library is the organizing principle

The portfolio is a library of named engineering patterns (chess openings), not a stack of case studies. Section content is sub-patterns.

Decision 03

CV-as-source-of-truth, always

Every claim traced to cv.md. No invented numbers, no fluff. The CV file is the canonical truth the build reads from.

Decision 04

Decentralized homepage

The homepage is no longer the only pitch surface. Every page carries a positioning line, at least one proof number, and a path back.

Decision 05

Static-first, additive schema

Vercel · SSG · markdown/JSON content · serverless dynamic · additive schema. Adding fields doesn't break older entries.

Decision 06

CV editable without redeploy

Content lives where the live site reads it. A save fires a per-page ISR regen — the build doesn't move when content does.

Decision 07

Leadership = primary proof vector

The CV under-leads leadership. Lead with the pushback story, the data-led persuasion, the standards-as-non-negotiable pattern.

Decision 08

Lab reframed professionally

New heading · Production / Experiment / Personal tags · micro-case-study shape. Lab is a portfolio surface, not a hobby drawer.
