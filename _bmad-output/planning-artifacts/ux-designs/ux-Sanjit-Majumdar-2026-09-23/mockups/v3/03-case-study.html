01 / Context

An operator running three ERPs and one spreadsheet.

The client — a mid-cap international oil & gas operator with upstream and downstream operations across three continents — was running on three legacy ERP systems acquired through a decade of M&A. Each one carried its own project, contract, and governance model. Reconciling a single cross-region project required a spreadsheet, a phone call, and an act of faith.

The board mandated consolidation. Not a rip-and-replace — the existing systems held too much institutional knowledge — but a unifying layer that gave every region one canonical view of every project, contract, and approval.

My role: lead a 7-person engineering team through the design and build of that layer. I worked directly with the CIO, the regional IT directors, and the audit committee chair. The platform shipped in 14 months and is now the operational backbone for 10,000+ daily users.

02 / Problem

Reconciliation was a full-time job nobody could hire for.

The core technical problem wasn't "build a CRUD app" — it was a data-integrity problem dressed as a software problem. Three ERPs, each with their own schema, their own IDs, their own version of "what does an active project look like." Reconciliation was done by a team of analysts working in Excel. They were brilliant, but the process was unscalable and unauditable.

Adding to the pressure: the operator's audit committee had given an 18-month window to retire the manual reconciliation process entirely. If we missed it, the committee would mandate a single-ERP rip-and-replace that nobody on the engineering side wanted.

Constraint

The legacy ERPs could not be retired — only read from. They owned the source of truth for finance, HR, and supply chain. Our platform had to treat them as authoritative, never overwrite.

03 / Approach

Build the canonical model; let the ERPs stay where they are.

We started by treating the three ERPs as systems of record and building a canonical model layer on top. Every night, reconciliation workers pulled deltas from each ERP, normalized them into our canonical schema, and flagged any conflicts for analyst review. The user-facing app always read from the canonical model — never from an ERP directly.

This worked because we made one hard rule early: the canonical model is the only writable surface. The ERPs are read-only from our perspective. Analysts can correct canonical records; corrections propagate back to ERPs through explicit, audited sync jobs.

We shipped iteratively — first a read-only reconciliation view, then write-back for the most common contract types, then governance workflows (approvals, audit trail, signoffs). Each phase was a six-week cycle with regional pilots before global rollout.

04 / Architecture

Read-heavy. Write-gated. Audit-everything.

The platform is built on .NET 5 with Angular 11 on the front end. SQL Server holds the canonical model; each ERP has a thin shim service that exposes its delta log. Reconciliation runs as scheduled Azure Functions, with conflict resolution surfacing in a dedicated Angular app for analysts.

Real-time updates (a contract amendment, an approval click) propagate to all viewers via SignalR. We tested this with 10K concurrent connections before the first migration window — load testing caught a fan-out bottleneck in the early SignalR hub config that would have collapsed production on day one.

Authentication is federated against the client's existing AAD; role definitions encode both regional and functional access. Every canonical-model mutation writes an immutable audit row that the audit committee can query directly.

05 / Decisions & tradeoffs

Three calls that shaped the platform.

Decision
Alternative
Why we chose it
Canonical model layer
Single-ERP consolidation
Avoided 24-month rip-and-replace. Kept institutional knowledge in source systems.
Nightly reconciliation
Real-time CDC streams
Auditable, predictable, lower infra cost. Stakeholders wanted 24h SLAs, not milliseconds.
Angular + .NET monolith
Microservices from day one
Team of 7 couldn't operate a microservices fleet. Single deployable matched team capacity.

The "monolith-first" call was the one that aged best. We had three engineers who could operate a single .NET deployment confidently. Asking the same team to operate eight microservices with separate CI, monitoring, and on-call rotations would have been a staffing problem disguised as an architecture decision.

06 / Measurable results

Receipts.

The platform shipped against an 18-month audit-committee deadline with two months to spare. Manual reconciliation is fully retired. The three legacy ERPs continue to run, but they're now treated as data sources rather than the authoritative truth — and the analysts who used to do reconciliation have moved into platform-adjacent roles (config, governance, audit) which are higher-leverage for the business.
